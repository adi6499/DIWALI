I · The first flame

Light, passed from lamp to lamp

Deepotsav (दीपोत्सव) means “festival of lamps”. Deepavali (दीपावली), or Diwali, means “a row of lamps”. It falls on Amavasya, the new-moon night, the darkest of the lunar month, usually in October or November.

Hindus, Jains and Sikhs, and many Buddhists in Nepal, each keep it for their own reasons. Keep scrolling: this journey follows those stories.

II · The way home · 1 of 4

Fourteen years

In the Ramayana, Prince Rama of Ayodhya accepts fourteen years of exile in the forest so that his father, King Dasharatha, can keep a promise he once made. Sita and Rama’s brother Lakshmana choose to go with him.

II · The way home · 2 of 4

Light over darkness

During the exile, Sita is abducted by Ravana, king of Lanka. With Hanuman and an army of allies, Rama crosses the sea, and after a long war Ravana is defeated. Many communities mark that victory as Dussehra, about twenty days before Diwali.

II · The way home · 3 of 4

The road to Ayodhya

Once the exile is over, Rama, Sita and Lakshmana travel home. In Valmiki’s telling they ride the flying chariot Pushpaka. Hanuman goes ahead with the news to Bharata, who has ruled in Rama’s name all those years with Rama’s sandals on the throne.

II · The way home · 4 of 4

A city of lamps

Tradition holds that the people of Ayodhya lit clay lamps along every path, rooftop and riverbank to welcome them home on that moonless night. Deepavali remembers that welcome. In Ayodhya today, millions of diyas are lit for Deepotsav on the banks of the Sarayu.

The Ramayana is told in hundreds of versions across South and Southeast Asia, and families link Diwali to different stories. Some of those stories follow.

III · What Diwali means

Welcoming Lakshmi

On the evening of Amavasya, families worship Lakshmi, goddess of prosperity, good fortune and well-being. She is usually worshipped together with Ganesha, who removes obstacles. In one well-known story, Lakshmi rose from the churning of the cosmic ocean.

Homes are cleaned and left open to the light. Rangoli is drawn at the threshold, often with small footprints leading inside to welcome the goddess, and lamps are kept burning into the night. In western India many business families begin a new financial year at this time, and bless their new account books in Chopda Pujan.

III · शुभ धनतेरस · Happy Dhanteras

Dhanteras

Dhanteras (Dhanatrayodashi) opens the five days of Diwali. Its name joins dhan, wealth, and teras, the thirteenth day of the waning moon, two days before Lakshmi Puja.

The physician of the gods. When the devas and asuras churned the cosmic ocean (the Samudra Manthan), Dhanvantari rose from the waters carrying a pot of amrita, the nectar of immortality. He is the physician of the gods and the deity of Ayurveda. Dhanteras is also kept as Dhanvantari Jayanti, a day to pray for good health, in the spirit of the saying “पहला सुख निरोगी काया”: the first happiness is a healthy body.

The lamp that kept Yama away. A popular legend tells of King Hima’s son, whose stars foretold death by snakebite on the fourth night of his marriage. That night his young bride heaped her gold and silver ornaments and coins at the door, lit lamps all around, and kept him awake with stories and songs. Yama, god of death, came in the form of a serpent, but the blaze of light dazzled him. He sat on the glittering heap listening until dawn, then quietly went away.

So on Dhanteras many families light a lamp for Yama (Yama Deepam) outside the home, facing south, the direction of Yama. They buy something new in gold, silver or metal, often utensils, and worship Lakshmi and Kubera, keeper of the treasures of the gods. At the threshold of the courtyard you can see a heap of gold and a four-wick lamp, one wick turned to the south.

III · Jain Diwali

The nirvana of Mahavira

For Jains, Diwali marks the nirvana (moksha) of Bhagwan Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara. According to Jain tradition he attained it at Pavapuri, in present-day Bihar, in 527 BCE.

The Kalpa Sutra says the assembled kings lit lamps that night: since the light of knowledge had gone, they would make a light of material things. The next day his chief disciple, Gautama Swami, attained kevala jnana (omniscience), and the Jain new year, Vira Nirvana Samvat, begins.

Many Jains keep the day with fasting, prayer and recitation of the Uttaradhyayana Sutra, believed to be Mahavira’s final teaching. Many also choose lamps over firecrackers out of ahimsa, care for every living being.

III · Sikh Diwali

Bandi Chhor Divas

Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, the “Day of Liberation”. In 1619 (sources differ slightly on the year), the sixth Guru, Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji, was freed from Gwalior Fort, where Emperor Jahangir had held him.

The Guru refused to leave unless 52 imprisoned kings were freed with him. He was told that only those who could hold on to his robe might go, so he had a cloak made with 52 tassels, and every one of them walked out with him.

When he reached Amritsar, the Harmandir Sahib was lit with lamps. It still is: every year the Golden Temple and its sarovar shine, and gurdwaras around the world gather in celebration.

III · The festival week

Five days of light

  1. Dhanteras: Dhanvantari and Lakshmi are honoured; buying metal is auspicious, and a lamp is lit for Yama.
  2. Naraka Chaturdashi (Choti Diwali): Krishna’s victory over the demon Narakasura. In the South, Maharashtra and Goa the day starts before dawn with an oil bath. In Tamil Nadu, this is Deepavali itself.
  3. Lakshmi Puja: the new-moon night of lamps, rangoli and sweets shared with neighbours. In Bengal, Assam and Odisha, Kali Puja is celebrated on this same night.
  4. Govardhan Puja · Bali Pratipada · New Year: Krishna lifting Govardhan hill to shelter the people of Braj, with temples offering Annakut, a “mountain of food”. In Maharashtra and Karnataka, the generous King Bali is remembered, couples celebrate Padwa, and Gujarati families welcome the New Year (Bestu Varas).
  5. Bhai Dooj: sisters pray for their brothers’ well-being and brothers give gifts. It is Bhau-beej in Maharashtra, Bhai Phonta in Bengal and Bhai Tika in Nepal.

Days follow the lunar calendar, and customs differ from region to region and family to family.

III · Across India and the world

One festival, many lights

  • Bengal, Assam, Odisha: Kali Puja on Amavasya. In Odisha, families burn jute sticks in Kaunria Kathi to light the way for ancestors.
  • Gujarat: Chopda Pujan for the new accounts, then the New Year with greetings of Saal Mubarak.
  • Maharashtra: trays of faral sweets and snacks, and clay forts built by children in memory of Shivaji’s forts.
  • Goa: giant Narakasura effigies paraded and burned before dawn.
  • Tamil Nadu: a dawn oil bath, new clothes and the greeting “Ganga snanam aacha?”, meaning “Have you had your Ganga bath?”
  • Punjab: Bandi Chhor Divas at the Harmandir Sahib.
  • Nepal: Tihar, five days honouring crows, dogs, cows and Lakshmi, Mha Puja among the Newars, and Bhai Tika.
  • Varanasi: fifteen days later, on Kartik Purnima, the ghats glow again for Dev Deepawali.
  • Around the world: a public holiday in Fiji, Guyana, Malaysia, Mauritius, Nepal, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Suriname and Trinidad and Tobago, and celebrated in cities from Leicester to Durban and Toronto.

Draw at the threshold

Rangoli welcomes guests, and on Diwali Lakshmi, at the door. The design on the courtyard floor is yours to redraw: mirrored symmetry, powder colours, petals and dots.
